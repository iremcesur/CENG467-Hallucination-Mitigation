\title{Mitigating Hallucinations via Epistemic Uncertainty:\\
A Self-Consistency and Entropy Filtering Approach}

\author{İrem Cesur}

\institute{Department of Computer Engineering,\\
Izmir Institute of Technology, Izmir, Turkey\\
\url{https://github.com/iremcesur/CENG467-Hallucination-Mitigation}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Hallucination in large language models (LLMs) remains a critical obstacle for
reliable deployment. We investigate whether \emph{epistemic uncertainty},
quantified via Shannon entropy over self-sampled responses, can serve as an
effective proxy for answer reliability. We evaluate three baselines on
TruthfulQA (817 questions) and HaluEval QA (200-sample subset) using
Llama-3.1-8B-Instant via the Groq API. Zero-shot prompting achieves 52.8\%
accuracy; self-consistency ($N{=}5$) achieves 53.0\%; entropy filtering
($t{=}0.1$) achieves 62.7\% accuracy at 73.2\% coverage, a $+9.9$
percentage-point gain. Entropy bucket analysis confirms the signal:
low-entropy questions ($H{<}0.3$) yield 60.3\% accuracy on TruthfulQA and
96.1\% on HaluEval, versus 33.3\% and 60.0\% for high-entropy questions
($H{\geq}0.7$). We further validate entropy with AUROC\,=\,0.640
(TruthfulQA) and 0.735 (HaluEval), and abstention F1\,=\,0.727 and 0.948.
Calibration analysis (ECE) shows the confidence signal is well calibrated on
HaluEval (0.076) but poorly calibrated on TruthfulQA (0.367); temperature
scaling reduces ECE by 82.0\% and 69.5\% respectively. We additionally test
semantic clustering as a refinement, finding it unexpectedly degrades
discriminative power on HaluEval (AUROC 0.735$\to$0.497) because sampled
responses are overwhelmingly near-paraphrases regardless of correctness.
These results show entropy-based abstention is a simple, training-free
mechanism that meaningfully reduces hallucination in black-box LLM settings,
while highlighting calibration as a necessary complement to raw entropy
filtering.
\keywords{Hallucination mitigation \and Epistemic uncertainty \and
Self-consistency \and Shannon entropy \and TruthfulQA \and HaluEval \and
AUROC \and Model calibration}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Large language models (LLMs) achieve strong performance across NLP tasks but
remain prone to hallucination — generating fluent, confident text that is
factually incorrect~\cite{ji2023survey}. This poses serious risks in
high-stakes domains such as healthcare, law, and education. A key challenge is
that LLMs produce point estimates without any accompanying uncertainty
signal: a model is equally fluent whether recalling a fact reliably or
confabulating a plausible-sounding falsehood.

This paper investigates whether \emph{epistemic uncertainty} — the model's
internal uncertainty about the correct answer — can be estimated cheaply and
used to filter unreliable responses. We adopt the self-consistency framework
of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}: sample $N{=}5$ independent
responses with stochastic decoding and compute the Shannon entropy of the
resulting vote distribution as an uncertainty proxy. Our hypothesis is that
low-entropy predictions (the model converges to the same answer across
samples) are more likely correct than high-entropy predictions (responses
scatter). If so, an entropy-based abstention policy should trade coverage for
accuracy.

We evaluate this on two complementary benchmarks: TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa}
(multiple-choice, targeting common misconceptions) and
HaluEval~\cite{ji2023halueval} (open-ended QA with paired correct/hallucinated
answers), using Llama-3.1-8B-Instant via the Groq API. Beyond the core
accuracy-coverage results, we validate entropy with standard
uncertainty-quantification metrics (AUROC, F1, ECE) and directly test two
proposed refinements — temperature scaling and semantic clustering — rather
than leaving them as unexplored future work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Ji et al.~\cite{ji2023survey} survey hallucination in NLG, distinguishing
intrinsic, extrinsic, and factual hallucination, and identify training data
quality and decoding strategy as key contributing factors; our work targets
the inference-time question of identifying likely hallucinations without gold
references. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency} introduced
self-consistency, sampling multiple reasoning paths and aggregating via
majority vote, improving accuracy by 10--20\% on reasoning benchmarks such as
GSM8K; we repurpose the resulting vote-distribution entropy as an uncertainty
signal rather than using sampling purely for accuracy gains. Kadavath et
al.~\cite{kadavath2022language} showed LLMs can verbalize calibrated
confidence to a limited extent, though this requires an extra inference pass
and can itself hallucinate. Kuhn et al.~\cite{kuhn2023semantic} proposed
semantic entropy, clustering semantically equivalent responses before
computing entropy to remove surface-form noise; we adopt syntactic majority
voting as our primary method (sufficient for multiple-choice and binary
correctness) and directly test semantic clustering as an extension in
Section~\ref{sec:extended-analyses}. More broadly, our entropy-filtering
approach is an instance of selective prediction~\cite{kadavath2022language},
where the confidence score derives from sampling variance rather than model
logits, making it applicable to black-box API settings.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Model, Infrastructure, and Datasets}

All experiments use Llama-3.1-8B-Instant via the Groq API, with exponential
backoff (45s) on rate-limit errors. \textbf{TruthfulQA}~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa}:
the multiple-choice (mc1) validation split, 817 questions across 38
misconception categories, average 5.04 choices per question (binary labels,
1\,=\,correct); we use mc1 for simplicity of majority-vote aggregation.
\textbf{HaluEval QA}~\cite{ji2023halueval}: 10{,}000 question-answer pairs
with supporting \texttt{knowledge}, \texttt{question}, \texttt{right\_answer},
and \texttt{hallucinated\_answer} fields; we randomly sample 200 examples
(seed\,=\,42). Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes both datasets.

\subsection{Baselines and Entropy Computation}

\textbf{Baseline 1 (Zero-Shot)} queries the model once per question at
temperature~$=0.0$, following the zero-shot chain-of-thought paradigm of
Kojima et al.~\cite{kojima2022large}, instructing it to respond with a single
letter. \textbf{Baseline 2 (Self-Consistency, $N{=}5$)} samples 5 independent
completions at temperature~$=0.7$ and takes the majority vote. Shannon entropy
over the vote distribution is:

\begin{equation}
  H = -\sum_{i} p(i) \log p(i), \qquad
  H_{\text{norm}} = \frac{H}{\log K}
  \label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $p(i)$ is the vote proportion for option $i$ and $K$ the
number of choices; normalizing keeps $H_{\text{norm}} \in [0,1]$ regardless of
$K$. \textbf{Baseline 3 (Entropy Filtering)} answers only when
$H_{\text{norm}} < t$, abstaining otherwise; we sweep $t \in \{0.1,\ldots,1.0\}$
and select $t{=}0.1$, which maximizes accuracy subject to coverage
$\geq 50\%$. For \textbf{HaluEval}, the model generates free-text answers;
correctness uses lexical matching (response must contain $\geq 50\%$ of the
reference answer's content words, length $>3$), and entropy is computed over
the binary correct/incorrect split across the 5 samples, normalized by
$\log 2$.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison and Entropy Buckets}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} summarizes the three baselines on TruthfulQA.
Self-consistency yields only a marginal $+0.2\%$ over zero-shot — expected,
since TruthfulQA is primarily factual recall rather than multi-step reasoning,
limiting the benefit of sample aggregation. Entropy filtering provides the
real gain: answering only the 598 questions (73.2\%) where the model is most
confident raises accuracy to 62.7\% ($+9.9$ points over zero-shot).

\subsection{Additional Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:additional-metrics}

Accuracy and coverage alone do not establish that entropy is a
\emph{statistically reliable} predictor or that it is \emph{calibrated}. We
report AUROC (discriminative power), Precision/Recall/F1 (the abstention
decision as binary classification), and Expected Calibration Error (ECE).

\textbf{AUROC.} Using $-H_{\text{norm}}$ as a score, we measure how well it
separates correct from incorrect responses (0.5\,=\,random, 1.0\,=\,perfect).
Table~\ref{tab:auroc} shows both datasets clear the random baseline, with
HaluEval's cleaner (near-binary) entropy distribution yielding stronger
separation. Figure~\ref{fig:roc} shows the full ROC curves.

\begin{table}[h]

\label{tab:auroc}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}

\midrule
TruthfulQA & 817 & 433 & 0.640 \\

\textbf{ECE.} We assess whether $1-H_{\text{norm}}$ is a well-calibrated
confidence score using 10 equal-width bins:
$\text{ECE} = \sum_{b} \frac{n_b}{N}\left|\text{acc}(b)-\text{conf}(b)\right|$.
HaluEval is reasonably well calibrated (0.076); TruthfulQA is not (0.367) —
confidence systematically overstates accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:ece},
Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}). This means entropy \emph{discriminates}
effectively (Table~\ref{tab:auroc}) but is not directly interpretable as a
calibrated probability on TruthfulQA.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error-analysis}

Among the 598 TruthfulQA questions answered at $t{=}0.1$, 375 are correct and
223 (37.3\%) are \textbf{false-confidence} errors: the model converges on a
wrong answer with full agreement across samples (Table~\ref{tab:errors}).
Manual inspection shows these consistently reflect intuitive but factually
wrong options — e.g., misattributing ``I cannot tell a lie'' or
misidentifying the world's smallest country — i.e., misconceptions
prevalent in the pretraining corpus itself, where low sampling variance
reflects consistency, not correctness. The complementary cost is
\textbf{high-entropy correct} cases: questions the model would have answered
correctly but where votes split enough to trigger abstention, visible in the
gap between Baseline 2's 53.0\% overall accuracy and the 86.6\% recall in
Table~\ref{tab:f1}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Outcome distribution on TruthfulQA at $t{=}0.1$ (598 answered).}
\label{tab:errors}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Outcome}                  & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{\% of answered} \\
\midrule
Correct                           & 375            & 62.7\%                  \\
False confidence (wrong, low $H$) & 223            & 37.3\%                  \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

This 37.3\% rate quantifies why calibration matters: entropy discriminates
well in aggregate (AUROC\,=\,0.640) but full sample agreement gives no further
signal to flag these residual errors — addressing them likely requires
orthogonal techniques such as retrieval augmentation or external fact
verification.

\subsection{Extended Analyses}
\label{sec:extended-analyses}

Rather than leaving calibration and semantic clustering as unexplored future
work, we test both directly using the existing sampled responses (no new API
calls).

\textbf{Temperature scaling.} We fit a single scalar $T$ minimizing
negative log-likelihood on the correctness labels, rescaling confidence as
$\sigma(\text{logit}(c)/T)$. This reduces ECE by 82.0\% on TruthfulQA and
69.5\% on HaluEval (Table~\ref{tab:tempscale}, Figure~\ref{fig:tempscale}).
The large fitted $T$ on TruthfulQA (27.23) indicates the raw confidence needed
substantial flattening toward the base accuracy rate — consistent with its
weaker AUROC relative to HaluEval.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Effect of temperature scaling on ECE.}
\label{tab:tempscale}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{Fitted $T$} & \textbf{ECE before} & \textbf{ECE after} \\
\midrule
TruthfulQA & 27.23 & 0.367 & 0.066 \\
HaluEval   & 4.43  & 0.076 & 0.023 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{temperature_scaling_calibration.png}
  \caption{Reliability diagrams before/after temperature scaling.}
  \label{fig:tempscale}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Semantic clustering.} Kuhn et al.~\cite{kuhn2023semantic} argue
semantically equivalent responses with different wording should count as
agreement. We embed each HaluEval sample's 5 responses with
\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} and apply agglomerative clustering (cosine
distance, threshold\,=\,0.3) before recomputing entropy over the cluster
distribution. The result is counter to expectation: semantic entropy
\emph{degrades} discrimination to near-random (Table~\ref{tab:semantic}).
Inspection shows the mean clusters per sample was only 1.07/5 — the model's
sampled responses are nearly always semantically equivalent paraphrases, even
when lexical matching marks the majority answer wrong. The model often
expresses the \emph{same} (sometimes incorrect) claim in different words, so
clustering collapses nearly every sample into one cluster regardless of
correctness, destroying the variance the syntactic signal relies on. For this
task, syntactic disagreement is itself informative about genuine content
uncertainty, and semantic clustering does not straightforwardly transfer the
benefits reported by Kuhn et al.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Syntactic vs.\ semantic entropy as a correctness predictor (HaluEval).}
\label{tab:semantic}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Entropy variant} & \textbf{AUROC} \\
\midrule
Syntactic (binary correct/incorrect) & 0.735 \\
Semantic (embedding clusters)        & 0.497 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Normalized Shannon entropy over self-sampled responses is a practical
hallucination proxy: when a model has reliably encoded a fact, its outputs
are robust to decoding perturbations (low entropy); when it must interpolate
between imperfectly learned patterns, sampling variance — and entropy — rise.

\textbf{Limitations.} (1) $N{=}5$ is a small ensemble relative to the
$N{=}20$--$40$ typical in prior work; this was constrained by Groq API rate
limits and would likely sharpen entropy estimates if increased. (2) The
HaluEval correctness criterion (lexical matching) is a noisy proxy for
semantic correctness; embedding-based similarity or an LLM judge would be
more robust. (3) 26.8\% of TruthfulQA questions are abstained at $t{=}0.1$,
requiring a fallback strategy in real deployment. (4) We directly tested
semantic clustering (Section~\ref{sec:extended-analyses}) and found it
\emph{underperforms} our syntactic approach on HaluEval (AUROC 0.735$\to$0.497),
since responses are overwhelmingly near-paraphrases; this finding may not
generalize to settings with more lexically diverse outputs. (5) Raw entropy-derived
confidence is poorly calibrated on TruthfulQA (ECE\,=\,0.367); we addressed
this with temperature scaling (Section~\ref{sec:extended-analyses}), cutting
ECE by 82.0\%/69.5\%, and recommend such post-hoc correction for applications
requiring calibrated probabilities rather than raw entropy scores.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Normalized Shannon entropy over self-sampled responses is an effective,
training-free proxy for epistemic uncertainty in LLMs. Entropy-based
abstention on TruthfulQA yields a $+9.9$ percentage-point accuracy gain over
zero-shot while answering 73.2\% of questions; on HaluEval the signal is
stronger still. Beyond accuracy-coverage, we validated entropy with AUROC
(0.640/0.735) and F1 (0.727/0.948), and directly tested two proposed
refinements rather than leaving them speculative: temperature scaling
substantially improved calibration (ECE $-82.0\%$/$-69.5\%$), while semantic
clustering unexpectedly degraded discrimination on HaluEval, since sampled
responses are overwhelmingly near-paraphrases regardless of correctness.

These results position entropy filtering as a practical first-line defense
against hallucination in black-box LLM deployments, but not a complete
solution: low entropy indicates the model is \emph{consistent}, not that it
is \emph{correct}, particularly on benchmarks like TruthfulQA that target
ingrained misconceptions — illustrated by the 37.3\% false-confidence rate
among answered questions. Remaining future work includes larger sample sizes
($N{\geq}20$, beyond our API budget here), combination with
retrieval-augmented generation or external fact verification to catch
high-confidence hallucinations that sampling-based uncertainty cannot detect,
and extension to generation tasks beyond factual QA.
